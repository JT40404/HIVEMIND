THE QUESTION

Smart colonies from simple minds.

A honeybee's brain is smaller than a sesame seed, yet a swarm of bees reliably picks the best new home out of many options. How?

No bee sees every option, and no bee is in charge. Each one gathers a little evidence, shares it through a dance, and gradually loses interest in sites that aren't worth the trip. HIVEMIND takes that process and puts it to work. After launch, a colony of bee-inspired agents decides what happens with the project's treasury, using the same explore, dance, and quorum process a swarm uses to choose a home.

01 / THE BEE BRAIN

Four systems, one tiny brain.

A honeybee brain has roughly a million neurons, organized into specialized regions. HIVEMIND borrows what each region does, not an exact copy of how it's wired.

MUSHROOM BODIES

Learning and memory.

Paired structures packed with Kenyon cells. This is where a bee links a scent or color to a reward and remembers which flowers paid off.

In the colony: each mind remembers the site it found and how good it judged it to be.

ANTENNAL LOBES

Sensing signals.

Around 160 small clusters called glomeruli turn smells into distinct activity patterns before passing them on to memory.

In the colony: resting minds sense how many dancers are on the dance floor and what they're advertising.

CENTRAL COMPLEX

Compass and steering.

Uses the sun and patterns of polarized skylight to hold a heading and keep a running total of the path flown, so a forager can fly straight home.

In the colony: every mind tracks its heading and distance from the hive. Select one to see it.

WAGGLE DANCE

Sharing a location.

A returning scout waggles her abdomen along a straight run. The run's angle gives the direction, and its length gives the distance.

In the colony: scouts dance for sites they like, and dance less each trip.

02 / DECODE A DANCE

Direction is an angle.
Distance is time.

Inside the dark hive, straight up on the comb stands in for the sun. A bee that waggles 40° to the right of vertical is saying the target is 40° to the right of the sun. The longer the waggle run lasts, the farther away it is, at roughly one second per kilometre, though the exact rate varies between colonies.

Between waggle runs the dancer loops back to the start, alternating left and right, tracing a figure eight. Followers pressed close to her pick up the angle and the timing.

    What the colony can choose.

    Each option is scored fresh at every gate, based on conditions at the time.

    THE 60/40 RULE

    Spend some, save some.

    At every gate the colony allocates 60% of the treasury according to its decision. The other 40% is retained and carried into the next gate, so the swarm never spends everything in one round.

    1 · SCOUT

    Each agent looks for itself.

    Agents independently review conditions and score the available treasury actions. No agent sees the whole picture, and none of them is in charge.

    2 · DANCE

    Strong cases get louder.

    Agents advertise the actions they rate highly to the rest of the colony. The better an agent rates an option, the harder it pushes for it.

    3 · CHECK

    Others verify before joining.

    Recruited agents score the option for themselves instead of copying. Support fades each round unless it keeps being earned, and rival agents can cut weak campaigns short.

    4 · QUORUM

    Enough agreement, then action.

    Once enough agents back the same action at the same time, the colony commits and the action is carried out. Every action is an on-chain transaction anyone can check.

    Why a swarm?

    Real swarms pick the best of several homes most of the time, without a leader. They do it by mixing independent judgment, competition, and a quorum. The same checks make it harder for one bad read or one loud voice to steer the treasury.

    05 / THE SCIENCE

    What it's built on.

    Everything on this page is a simplified version of well-studied honeybee biology. Here's where each piece comes from, in plain terms.

    THE WAGGLE DANCE

    Karl von Frisch

    Worked out that bees encode direction and distance in their dance, research that won a Nobel Prize in 1973. The decoder above uses his core rules.

    SWARM DECISIONS

    Thomas Seeley

    Showed how swarms choose a nest site through competing dances, a steady decline in each scout's dancing, and a quorum at the chosen site. The colony simulation follows this process.

    STOP SIGNALS

    Cross-inhibition

    Scouts backing one site head-butt dancers backing another, cutting their dances short. This helps a swarm break ties and avoid deadlock, similar to how rival neuron groups inhibit each other in a brain.

    LEARNING

    Mushroom bodies

    Decades of work on how bees learn to link smells with sugar rewards point to the mushroom bodies as a central hub for associative memory.

    NAVIGATION

    Central complex

    Models of the bee central complex show how a small circuit can keep a compass heading and sum up an outbound path, which is enough to steer straight home.

    WHAT THIS ISN'T

    An honest scope

    The agents borrow how bee brains and swarms work. They aren't neuron-by-neuron brain simulations.
